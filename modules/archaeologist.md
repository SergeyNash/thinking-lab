# Archaeologist

The Archaeologist finds what is strange, durable, or worth investigating.

It turns an artifact or rough topic into a research question and tension.

---

## 1. Purpose

The Archaeologist starts the research process.

It asks:

> What is interesting here that is not explained by the obvious story?

---

## 2. What It Does

The Archaeologist:

- identifies the artifact or phenomenon;
- looks for strangeness, durability, failure, or recurring behavior;
- rejects shallow topic framing;
- formulates an explanatory question;
- names the initial tension;
- suggests why the topic may reveal a transferable principle.

---

## 3. What It Does Not Do

The Archaeologist does not:

- write the article;
- prove the hypothesis;
- reconstruct full history;
- evaluate all evidence;
- produce final conclusions;
- decide publication format.

---

## 4. Inputs

Typical inputs:

- topic candidate from `knowledge/TOPICS.md`;
- rough intuition;
- concrete artifact;
- recurring pattern;
- surprising failure;
- durable technology or practice;
- reader or peer question.

---

## 5. Outputs

Expected outputs:

- artifact or phenomenon;
- why it is interesting;
- initial research question;
- obvious explanations to challenge;
- candidate tension;
- possible principle direction;
- handoff to Historian.

---

## 6. Procedure

1. Name the concrete artifact or phenomenon.
2. Ask what is strange, durable, repeated, or underexplained.
3. List obvious explanations.
4. Mark which obvious explanations feel incomplete.
5. Formulate a stronger "why" question.
6. Identify the system boundary that may matter.
7. Suggest what kind of principle might be hiding underneath.

---

## 7. Quality Checks

Before handoff, ask:

- Is there a concrete artifact?
- Is the question explanatory, not descriptive?
- Is there real tension?
- Are obvious explanations named?
- Could this reveal a transferable principle?
- Is this more than a content idea?

---

## 8. Handoff to Next Module

Handoff to Historian should include:

```markdown
Artifact:
Initial question:
Why interesting:
Obvious explanations:
Tension:
Suggested boundary:
What history/context is needed:
```

---

## 9. Failure Modes

Common failures:

- choosing a topic because it sounds publishable;
- framing the work as "what is X?";
- mistaking popularity for interestingness;
- starting with a conclusion;
- turning the artifact into a listicle;
- ignoring the obvious explanation that must be challenged.

---

## 10. Smoke Test: Git

For "Why did Git win?", the Archaeologist should identify:

- artifact: Git as version control and collaboration system;
- tension: Git did not win only because it was fast or because GitHub existed;
- question: why did Git fit the real behavior of software development?;
- possible principle: lowering the cost of experimentation changes system
  behavior.
