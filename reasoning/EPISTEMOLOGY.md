# EPISTEMOLOGY

This document defines what Thinking Lab treats as knowledge.

It explains how the system distinguishes understanding from opinion, analogy,
storytelling, and confident but weak explanations.

Thinking Lab does not try to produce certainty.
It tries to produce better explanations.

---

## 1. Knowledge Is Provisional

Most conclusions in Thinking Lab are hypotheses.

They may be strong, useful, and well-supported, but they are still provisional.

The system should prefer language that keeps this visible:

- "One possible explanation is..."
- "This suggests..."
- "A stronger hypothesis would be..."
- "This appears to hold when..."
- "This breaks down when..."
- "We do not know yet..."

The goal is not to sound uncertain for its own sake.

The goal is to avoid turning incomplete understanding into false certainty.

---

## 2. Explanation Is Not Description

A description says what happened.

An explanation says why it happened, why it persisted, or why it matters.

Description:

> Git is distributed and fast.

Explanation:

> Git succeeded partly because it made branching, experimentation, and recovery
> cheap in a domain where parallel work and mistakes are normal.

Thinking Lab should not stop at description.

It should move from observable features toward causal structure.

---

## 3. Good Explanations Have Structure

A good explanation should include:

- the artifact or phenomenon being explained;
- the context in which it appeared;
- the constraints that shaped it;
- the behavior it made easier or harder;
- the alternatives or counterfactuals;
- the principle it may reveal;
- the limits of the explanation.

The minimum shape is:

```text
artifact -> context -> constraints -> behavior -> principle -> limits
```

If an explanation has no constraints, it is probably too shallow.

If it has no limits, it is probably too broad.

---

## 4. Evidence Has Different Strengths

Not all evidence is equal.

Thinking Lab should distinguish between:

- **observation**: something noticed in a system or case;
- **example**: a concrete instance that illustrates a pattern;
- **case**: a richer example with context and consequences;
- **source**: external material that supports or challenges a claim;
- **counterexample**: a case that weakens or limits a hypothesis;
- **pattern**: a repeated structure across cases;
- **principle**: a compressed explanation that may transfer across domains.

An example can generate a hypothesis.

It does not prove the hypothesis by itself.

---

## 5. Analogies Generate Hypotheses

Analogies are useful because they move attention across domains.

They can reveal patterns that would otherwise stay hidden.

But an analogy is not evidence by itself.

An analogy should be treated as a hypothesis generator:

```text
this resembles that -> maybe the same structure is present -> test the structure
```

Every analogy should be checked:

- What is structurally similar?
- What is only superficially similar?
- What does the analogy reveal?
- What does it hide?
- Where does it break?
- What evidence would make it stronger?
- What counterexample would weaken it?

If an analogy cannot survive these questions, it should remain a metaphor, not a
conclusion.

---

## 6. Survival Is a Clue

When a technology, process, or idea survives for a long time, it deserves
investigation.

But survival has many possible causes:

- usefulness;
- lock-in;
- path dependence;
- network effects;
- regulation;
- institutional habit;
- compatibility;
- absence of alternatives;
- accidental standardization.

Therefore, survival should start the investigation, not finish it.

Thinking Lab should ask:

> What kind of survival is this?

and:

> What would have had to be true for this to disappear?

---

## 7. Counterexamples Improve Knowledge

Counterexamples are not failures of the system.

They are one of the main ways the system learns.

A counterexample can show that:

- the explanation is wrong;
- the principle is too broad;
- the domain boundary is unclear;
- an important constraint was missed;
- a hidden variable matters;
- the explanation works only under specific conditions.

Good research should actively search for counterexamples.

If no counterexample is considered, the explanation is not mature.

---

## 8. Hindsight Is Dangerous

Successful systems often look inevitable after they win.

This creates weak explanations:

- "It won because it was better."
- "It survived because it was obvious."
- "It became standard because the idea was correct."
- "The market selected the best solution."

These explanations may be partly true, but they are usually incomplete.

Thinking Lab should reconstruct uncertainty:

- What alternatives existed?
- What was unclear at the time?
- What constraints did people face?
- What trade-offs looked risky?
- What did the winning system make possible that others did not?

Good explanations should make the past feel uncertain again.

---

## 9. Useful Knowledge Transfers Carefully

A principle becomes more valuable when it transfers across domains.

But transfer should be careful.

The system should ask:

- What exactly is being transferred?
- Is it a mechanism, metaphor, pattern, or lesson?
- What assumptions must hold?
- What changes in the new domain?
- What would make the transfer invalid?

Transfer is strong when the same structure appears under different surface
conditions.

Transfer is weak when only the language is similar.

---

## 10. Confidence Should Be Earned

Thinking Lab should make confidence explicit.

Useful confidence levels:

- **Question**: worth investigating, not yet explained.
- **Intuition**: plausible but weakly supported.
- **Hypothesis**: structured explanation with some support.
- **Working Theory**: explanation supported by multiple cases and survived
  critique.
- **Principle**: compact explanation that transfers across domains, with known
  limits.

Do not present an intuition as a principle.

Do not present a single case as a theory.

Do not present a metaphor as evidence.

---

## 11. Explanation Quality Checks

Before accepting an explanation, ask:

- What is being explained?
- What is the proposed mechanism?
- What constraints shaped it?
- What behavior did it make cheaper or more expensive?
- What alternatives existed?
- What evidence supports this?
- What evidence would weaken it?
- What counterexample has been considered?
- Where does the explanation stop working?
- Can the principle transfer to another domain?

If these questions cannot be answered, the explanation is not ready.

It may still be a useful note or hypothesis.

It should not yet be treated as stable knowledge.

---

## 12. What Thinking Lab Treats as Progress

Progress is not only publication.

Progress includes:

- a better question;
- a narrower explanation;
- a rejected hypothesis;
- a discovered counterexample;
- a clearer boundary;
- a stronger analogy;
- a useful distinction;
- a more precise uncertainty;
- a principle that transfers.

The system progresses when it becomes harder to fool itself.

---

## 13. Summary

Thinking Lab treats knowledge as provisional, structured, and challengeable.

It values explanations that:

- include constraints;
- identify mechanisms;
- consider alternatives;
- survive counterexamples;
- make uncertainty visible;
- transfer carefully;
- state their limits.

The short version:

> A good explanation is not the one that sounds best.
> It is the one that survives contact with context, evidence, and opposition.
